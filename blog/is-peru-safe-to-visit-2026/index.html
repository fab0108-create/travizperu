Is Peru Safe to Visit in 2026? A Practical Guide

Photo: Jorge Láscar from Australia / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0.

By Francisco Arriz Barrantes · Founder, TravizPeru · APOTUR Certified AP030-11 · Updated 22 October 2026

Short answer: yes, millions of travelers visit Peru every year, and the classic route (Lima, Cusco, the Sacred Valley and Machu Picchu) is well established for tourism. But "safe" depends on where you go, how you move around and how well you prepare. This guide covers what the official advisory says, what the real risks are, and how to reduce them.

What the official advisory says

The U.S. Department of State currently rates Peru at Level 2: Exercise increased caution, citing crime, terrorism, civil unrest and kidnapping (advisory issued September 4, 2026). Some specific areas carry Level 4: Do Not Travel, including:

  • The Colombia–Peru border area in the Loreto Region
  • The VRAEM (Apurímac, Ene and Mantaro river valleys), including Vilcabamba
  • Abancay, Ayacucho, Huancavelica and Huancayo

The same advisory notes that the city of Cusco and nearby sites, including the Sacred Valley, the Inca Trail and Machu Picchu, are not located in the affected area. Always check the current advisory from your own government before booking, since these ratings change.

Where tourists usually go, and how it compares

  • Lima: Large, busy capital. Most visitors stay in Miraflores, Barranco or San Isidro. These districts are the usual choice for tourists, with more security presence, but petty theft can still happen.
  • Cusco and the Sacred Valley: The main gateway to Machu Picchu. Petty theft and taxi issues are the most common complaints.
  • Machu Picchu / Aguas Calientes: A controlled-access site with ticketing and ID checks.
  • Lake Titicaca (Puno) and the Amazon lodges: Typically visited with an organized operator.

The real risks (and what to do about them)

1. Petty theft

Pickpocketing and bag snatching are the most common problems for visitors, especially in crowded markets, buses and tourist squares.

  • Carry only what you need for the day.
  • Keep your phone out of sight on the street, and don't leave bags on the back of a chair.
  • Use a hotel safe for your passport and spare cards.

2. Transport

  • Avoid hailing taxis off the street. Ask your hotel to call a trusted driver, or arrange a private transfer in advance, especially for airport pickups at night.
  • For long distances, domestic flights are common and save many hours of road travel.
  • Road conditions in the mountains can be difficult. Choose reputable operators over the cheapest option.

3. Altitude (the one most travelers underestimate)

Cusco sits at around 3,400 m (11,150 ft). Machu Picchu is lower, at about 2,430 m (7,970 ft). Altitude sickness is one of the most common health issues for visitors.

  • Plan your first days at a gentler pace, and consider sleeping in the Sacred Valley (lower than Cusco) first.
  • Drink water, go easy on alcohol and heavy meals at first, and rest.
  • Talk to your doctor before the trip about altitude, especially if you have heart or lung conditions.

4. Scams

Typical examples include unofficial "guides" at tourist sites, overpriced offers from touts and fake tickets. Buy Machu Picchu tickets only from the official sales channel or from a licensed agency, and book through companies you can verify.

5. Unrest and strikes

Protests and road blockades have affected travel in parts of Peru in the past, sometimes causing delays and cancellations. Keep your plan flexible, buy travel insurance and ask your operator how they handle disruptions.

Practical safety checklist

  1. Buy travel insurance that covers medical care and evacuation.
  2. Check your government's travel advisory and register your trip if your country offers it.
  3. Make copies of your passport and keep them separate from the original.
  4. Pre-book transfers, especially on arrival.
  5. Plan time to acclimatize before Machu Picchu.
  6. Stay in well-reviewed hotels in established tourist districts.
  7. Ask your operator for an emergency contact available during the trip.

Is Peru safe for solo travelers, couples and families?

Many travelers visit alone, as couples or as families. The same principles apply: stay in recommended areas, use trusted transport and keep a flexible plan. Traveling with a private guide and pre-arranged transfers can make a trip simpler, since someone local handles logistics, timing and the details.
