Peru Itinerary: How to Plan 10 Days in Peru

By Francisco Arriz Barrantes · Founder, TravizPeru · APOTUR Certified AP030-11 · Updated 22 October 2026

Peru is large and varied, and most first-time visitors try to fit too much into too little time. This guide gives you a realistic 10-day route, explains how many days you need, and covers the planning details that matter most: altitude, Machu Picchu entry rules and transport.

How many days do you need in Peru?

  • 7 days: Lima, Cusco, Sacred Valley and Machu Picchu, at a fast pace.
  • 10 days: The sweet spot for a first visit. Enough time to acclimatize and add a second region.
  • 14+ days: Adds Lake Titicaca, Arequipa and Colca Canyon, or the Amazon.

If you can only choose one number, 10 days lets you see the highlights without rushing.

Sample 10-day Peru itinerary

This is our real tailor-made 10-day Peru itinerary, summarized day by day. It runs from sea level in Lima up through the Sacred Valley and Machu Picchu, and ends at Lake Titicaca. A starting rhythm, not a fixed package.

Day 1

Lima, arrival

Private transfer from the airport, Miraflores and an early night.

Day 2

Lima, properly

Huaca Pucllana, Barranco or the historic center, and one meal reserved ahead.

Day 3

Fly to Cusco, sleep in the valley

A short flight, then the drive down into the Sacred Valley and a light afternoon.

Day 4

Sacred Valley

Pisac's terraces and either the market or Chinchero's weavers, or Maras and Moray instead of one of them.

Day 5

Ollantaytambo and the train

Morning at the living Inca town, then the train to Aguas Calientes for the night.

Day 6

Machu Picchu

Your guide, your circuit and entry time, then the train back toward the valley or Cusco.

Day 7

Cusco, or a slow day

The city if you have the energy, a quiet hotel day if you do not.

Day 8

Cusco to Puno

About 10 hours by road with stops on the altiplano, or the scenic train on selected days.

Day 9

The lake

The Uros islands, then Taquile.

Day 10

Fly out of Juliaca

About 45 minutes from Puno to Juliaca, then a flight, usually through Lima.

Tip: Direction and order can change depending on train schedules and flights. A tailor-made route avoids backtracking.

How to get to Machu Picchu

Most travelers reach Machu Picchu by train from Cusco or the Sacred Valley to Aguas Calientes, then a short bus ride up to the site. Others walk a trek such as the Inca Trail, which has its own permit system and sells out far in advance.

Machu Picchu rules to know for 2026

According to Peru's state news agency Andina:

  • Daily visitor limits are 5,600 per day in high season (including June 19 to November 2, January 1, April 2-5 and December 30-31) and 4,500 per day the rest of the year.
  • Tickets are sold through the official site, tuboleto.cultura.pe.
  • You must present valid ID or your passport at entry.
  • There are several circuits (Panoramic, Classic and Royalty). Which one you choose affects what you can see.
  • Climbs such as Huayna Picchu and Machu Picchu Mountain use separate, limited tickets.

Rules change often, so confirm current requirements when you book. Booking your tickets, train and hotels together, well ahead of time, is the safest approach.

When is the best time to go?

The dry season (roughly May to September) is the most popular and has the clearest weather. The rainy season (roughly November to March) is greener and quieter, but can bring disruptions. Tickets and trains are more limited in high season, so plan early.

Altitude: the part that catches people out

Cusco is high. Give yourself time to adjust, drink water, eat lightly at first and avoid overplanning your first days. Ask your doctor about altitude before you travel.

Self-guided or private?

You can plan everything yourself, but Peru has many moving parts: flights, trains with limited seats, timed tickets and altitude. A private itinerary puts a single contact in charge of the logistics so you can focus on the trip.
