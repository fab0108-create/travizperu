How this line works

This note is about the railway that connects Cusco and the Sacred Valley with Machu Picchu. It is not a status report for flights, the road to Puno, or the citadel ticket.

The usual ride is from Ollantaytambo, in the Sacred Valley, to Aguas Calientes, also called Machu Picchu Pueblo. PeruRail publishes that leg at roughly an hour and a half to two hours, depending on the service. From the town, a shuttle bus climbs to the citadel gate. Some people walk the steep path instead. Some PeruRail services start in Cusco and meet the train farther down the valley. There is no second track to pass on.

Two companies carry passengers on that track: PeruRail and Inca Rail. They arrive at the same station in Aguas Calientes. A problem with one train can hold the other, which is what happened on 2 October and again, in a different way, on 5 October.

What usually interrupts it

Three kinds of stoppage show up on this line. I am describing the pattern, not predicting your week.

  • Single-track incidents. A collision, a breakdown, or a train that cannot move blocks everyone behind it. The 30 December 2025 collision and the two October 2026 interruptions were this kind. They were cleared in hours, except the December crash, which was far more serious and still saw service resume the next day.
  • Rain, January through March. Those are the wettest months in the Andes. Heavy rain and landslides in this gorge are a known reason the railway stops in rainy season. I do not have a landslide closure to report as of 8 October 2026.
  • Strikes and protests on the rails. Because trains cannot go around, a strike or a crowd on the track stops the line. On 5 October the stoppage lasted longer because passengers stood on the rails after the breakdown. That is a real example, not a rumor of a separate strike.
