How you actually get there

The citadel sits at about 2,430 meters, above the town of Aguas Calientes, also called Machu Picchu Pueblo. There is no road from Cusco and no ticket window that matters on the morning itself. Almost everyone arrives by train to the town, then takes the shuttle bus up to the gate, or walks the steep path if they would rather not ride.

PeruRail and Inca Rail both run that railway. The usual boarding point on a private trip is Ollantaytambo, in the Sacred Valley, about an hour and a half to two hours from Aguas Calientes depending on the service. Some services start in Cusco. I pick the departure that matches your entry time, not the one that is simply left over.

At the gate you need the official ticket for your circuit and your time, plus your passport. Peru's Ministry of Culture recommends visiting with a licensed guide. That is how I arrange it: your guide, your slot, and no shared group. A guide was required for a time; the rule now in force recommends one. I still would not send you in without one. The site is large, the circuits are one-way, and the story is the reason for being there.

Sleeping in Aguas Calientes the night before is the calm version. A same-day return to the valley is possible when the entry is not at dawn and the trains line up. I will tell you which of the two fits your dates instead of pretending both are equal.

Best time to visit Machu Picchu

The dry season (May–September) offers the clearest views but the busiest trails and highest hotel demand. Shoulder months (April, October, November) give you a strong balance of good weather and fewer crowds. Whatever the season, entrance tickets should be secured 2–4 months ahead — closer to peak season, some circuits sell out even earlier.

Daily entries are capped, and each ticket is for a specific circuit and time. I explain the practical side of that calendar in how many days you need in Peru. The railway is a single track. It runs, and it occasionally pauses.
